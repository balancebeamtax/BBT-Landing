# Tax-Ready Bookkeeping: What Small Business Owners Need Before Filing

Tax-ready bookkeeping means your books are organized enough for a tax preparer to prepare the return with confidence. It does not mean every bookkeeping file is perfect. It means the financial reports are complete, reconciled, and supported by records.

For small business owners, tax preparation often slows down because the books are not ready. The return may be extended, not because the tax form is complicated, but because the bookkeeping still needs attention.

An extension can help with filing time, but it does not create clean books. The IRS explains that an extension gives more time to file, while tax owed is generally still due by the original due date ([IRS extension guidance](https://www.irs.gov/filing/get-an-extension-to-file-your-tax-return)).

## What tax-ready bookkeeping includes

Tax-ready bookkeeping should give the preparer a clear view of the business activity for the year. At a minimum, that usually includes:

- Complete income records.
- Categorized business expenses.
- Reconciled bank and credit card accounts.
- Clear owner contributions, draws, distributions, or reimbursements.
- Loan balances and interest separated from principal.
- Payroll records, if applicable.
- Contractor payment records, if applicable.
- Sales tax, inventory, and asset records, if applicable.
- A profit and loss report.
- A balance sheet.
- Supporting statements and documents for unusual or large items.

The exact records depend on the business structure and activity. A Schedule C consultant with one bank account will not have the same bookkeeping needs as an S corporation with payroll, loans, credit cards, and contractors.

## Why reconciliation matters

Reconciliation is one of the clearest signs that bookkeeping is ready for tax preparation. If bank and credit card accounts are not reconciled, the reports may not reflect what actually happened.

Unreconciled books can hide:

- Duplicate transactions.
- Missing income.
- Missing expenses.
- Transfers recorded as income.
- Credit card payments recorded as expenses.
- Old transactions that never cleared.
- Personal activity mixed with business activity.

A tax preparer may still be able to start the return, but unreconciled records usually lead to follow-up questions, delays, and cleanup work.

## What a tax preparer needs from the books

A preparer needs more than a year-end profit and loss report. They need confidence that the report can be trusted.

For many small businesses, the preparer will need to understand:

- Whether all business accounts are included.
- Whether all income was recorded.
- Whether expenses are business-related and categorized properly.
- Whether owner payments are treated correctly.
- Whether payroll or contractor records match the books.
- Whether assets and loans are reflected properly.
- Whether the balance sheet contains old or unusual balances.

If the books cannot answer those questions, the return may need cleanup before filing.

## Tax-ready bookkeeping by entity type

Different business structures create different bookkeeping issues.

### Schedule C filers and single-member LLCs

Schedule C filers report business income and expenses with their individual return. The books should clearly separate business and personal activity, identify deductible business expenses, and support the year-end profit and loss report.

If a Schedule C filer requested an extension, the extended individual filing deadline is generally October 15 ([IRS extension guidance](https://www.irs.gov/filing/get-an-extension-to-file-your-tax-return)).

### Partnerships and multi-member LLCs

Partnerships generally need books that support Form 1065 and partner allocations. Capital accounts, distributions, guaranteed payments, loans, and partner contributions may need review.

For calendar-year partnerships with timely extensions, the IRS third-quarter tax calendar lists September 15, 2026 for extended Form 1065 filing ([IRS third-quarter tax calendar](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/third-quarter-tax-calendar)).

### S corporations

S corporations often need additional bookkeeping review around payroll, shareholder distributions, loans, reimbursements, and balance sheet accounts. A year-end profit and loss report is useful, but the balance sheet matters too.

For calendar-year S corporations with timely extensions, the IRS third-quarter tax calendar lists September 15, 2026 for extended Form 1120-S filing ([IRS third-quarter tax calendar](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/third-quarter-tax-calendar)).

## Signs your books are not tax-ready yet

Your books may need cleanup before filing if:

- Accounts have not been reconciled through year end.
- You have transactions in uncategorized income or uncategorized expense.
- You used “Ask My Accountant” as a holding category.
- Your balance sheet has negative, old, or unfamiliar balances.
- Business and personal transactions are mixed.
- Loan payments are coded entirely to expense.
- Payroll or contractor reports do not match the accounting file.
- You cannot explain major changes from last year.

None of these issues are unusual. They simply need time and review.
